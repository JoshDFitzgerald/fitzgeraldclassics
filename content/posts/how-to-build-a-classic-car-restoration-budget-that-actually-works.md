Why the purchase price may be the least important number in your restoration

There is a dangerous moment in almost every classic car restoration.

It usually happens shortly after you bring the car home.

You’re standing in the garage looking at your new project, imagining the finished car. The paint is perfect. The chrome shines. The engine compartment looks factory fresh. The interior smells new.

Then you start making a list.

Paint.

Chrome.

Interior.

Brakes.

Suspension.

Weatherstripping.

Wiring.

Tires.

Engine work.

Hardware.

Suddenly the inexpensive classic you were excited about buying doesn’t seem quite so inexpensive anymore.

That’s when the restoration really begins.

Not when you pick up the wrench.

## When you build the budget.

## The Purchase Price Is Only the Admission Ticket

One of the biggest mistakes people make when buying their first restoration project is focusing almost entirely on the purchase price.

A $5,000 car can easily become more expensive than a $20,000 car.

Why?

Because you’re not really buying a car.

You’re buying everything the car still needs.

Consider two hypothetical cars.

Car A costs $6,000 but needs:

Rust repair

Complete interior

Chrome

Wiring

Brakes

Tires

Engine work

Paint

Car B costs $15,000 but has a solid body, good chrome, a usable interior and a recently rebuilt drivetrain.

Car A feels like the bargain.

Car B may actually be far cheaper.

That’s why I try to evaluate a project based on its total path to completion, not simply the price written on the bill of sale.

## Start With the Finished Car

Before creating a budget, decide what you’re actually building.

This sounds obvious, but it’s incredibly important.

Are you building:

## A concours restoration?

## A high-quality driver?

## A weekend cruiser?

## A preservation car?

## A car you eventually intend to sell?

Each requires a completely different financial strategy.

My 1966 Corvette and my 1957 Metropolitan, for example, don’t have identical objectives.

With a highly documented, numbers-matching Corvette, originality can directly affect desirability and value. Correct finishes, date-coded components and factory details deserve considerable attention.

A Metropolitan being built as an attractive, dependable driver presents different opportunities.

Both cars deserve quality work.

But quality and concours correctness aren’t always the same thing.

Understanding that distinction can save thousands of dollars.

## The 20 Percent Rule

If I believe a project will require $20,000 to finish, I don’t mentally budget $20,000.

I budget closer to $24,000.

Why?

Because almost every restoration contains surprises.

You remove a carpet and discover rust.

You rebuild the brakes and discover worn wheel bearings.

You remove the transmission and find the clutch needs replacement.

You restore the dashboard and suddenly the tired steering wheel looks terrible beside it.

Restoration creates a domino effect.

My recommendation is to maintain at least a 15–20 percent contingency reserve.

For unknown or incomplete cars, I’d consider even more.

If you never spend it, terrific.

But you’ll be glad it’s there when the inevitable surprise appears.

## Beware of “While I’m Here”

Three of the most expensive words in classic car restoration are:

## While I’m here…

You’re replacing the clutch.

“While I’m here, I might as well rebuild the transmission.”

The transmission comes out.

“While I’m here, I should restore the driveshaft.”

Then the differential looks tired.

Then the underside suddenly doesn’t look good enough.

None of these decisions are necessarily wrong.

In fact, sometimes doing additional work while everything is accessible is financially smart.

The danger comes when project creep becomes automatic.

Before adding work, ask yourself:

Does this need to be done?

Is doing it now substantially easier or cheaper?

Does it support the goal I established for this car?

If the answer to all three is yes, proceed.

If not, put it on the future-project list.

## Spend Money Where You Can’t Easily Fix Mistakes

There are areas where I am perfectly comfortable doing the work myself.

Cleaning parts.

Painting brackets.

Replacing hardware.

Rebuilding simple assemblies.

Researching finishes.

Installing components.

There are other jobs where experience matters enormously.

Bodywork is a perfect example.

A beautiful paint job cannot hide poor metalwork underneath it.

The same applies to machining an engine, rebuilding certain transmissions, plating expensive original chrome and performing specialized upholstery work.

One of the smartest restoration decisions isn’t learning how to do everything.

It’s learning which jobs you shouldn’t do yourself.

## Don’t Automatically Replace Original Parts

This is one of the lessons my Corvette has reinforced repeatedly.

A dirty, rusty or worn-looking component isn’t necessarily junk.

Before throwing an original part away, clean it.

Inspect it.

Find the casting number.

Look for a date code.

Research it.

Determine whether it can be rebuilt.

The original component may be considerably more valuable—and sometimes better made—than the reproduction you’re about to order.

I’ve found that restoration often isn’t about buying new parts.

It’s about recognizing which old parts deserve to be saved.

## The $20 Part That Costs $200

Shipping can quietly destroy a restoration budget.

Order a $12 gasket Monday.

A $16 bracket Wednesday.

A $9 clip set Friday.

Pay shipping three times.

Repeat that process for two years and the numbers become surprisingly large.

Once I know I’m ordering from a particular supplier, I try to review the next several stages of the project and combine purchases whenever practical.

It requires patience.

But restoration rewards planning.

## Keep a Restoration Ledger

This may be the least exciting advice in this article.

It’s also some of the most valuable.

Record everything.

Purchase price.

Parts.

Shipping.

Machine work.

Paint.

Outside labor.

Even seemingly insignificant expenses.

You don’t need complicated accounting software. A spreadsheet works perfectly well.

Your ledger does more than tell you how much you’ve spent.

It helps you recognize patterns.

Maybe chrome is consuming more of the budget than expected.

Maybe you’re spending too much on expedited shipping.

Maybe a particular phase is approaching its allocation.

Information allows you to adjust before the budget gets away from you.

## Don’t Confuse Restoration Cost With Vehicle Value

This can be a painful lesson.

Spending $50,000 restoring a car does not automatically make it worth $50,000 more.

The market doesn’t reimburse us for our invoices.

Certain cars can justify extensive restorations financially.

Others cannot.

That doesn’t mean they shouldn’t be restored.

Some projects are about history.

Some are about family.

Some are about the challenge.

And some simply make us smile.

Those are perfectly legitimate reasons to restore an automobile.

Just understand the difference between an investment decision and an emotional decision.

Problems arise when we make an emotional decision while pretending it’s an investment.

## Fitzgerald Insight

I’ve come to believe that the best restoration budget isn’t the one you follow perfectly.

It’s the one that forces you to stop and think before spending money.

Every restoration will change.

You’ll discover things.

You’ll change your mind.

You’ll find an original component you weren’t expecting.

You’ll occasionally spend more than planned.

That’s part of the experience.

The goal isn’t to remove emotion from restoration. The emotion is why most of us love this hobby in the first place.

The goal is to prevent today’s excitement from becoming tomorrow’s regret.

Before ordering a part, I increasingly ask myself three questions:

## Do I actually need it?

## Is it correct for the car?

## Is there an original component I can restore instead?

Those three questions have saved me money—and, more importantly, helped me build better cars.

## The Number That Matters Most

There is one final number every restorer should know before starting a project.

Not what the car is worth today.

Not what you paid for it.

Not even what you think the restoration will cost.

Ask yourself:

## What am I comfortable having invested in this car when it’s finished?

That number creates boundaries.

Maybe the project ultimately exceeds the car’s market value.

That’s okay—provided you made that decision knowingly.

Classic automobiles aren’t stocks.

They’re pieces of history we can touch, drive, repair and eventually pass along.

There is value in that experience that will never appear in a price guide.

But there is also tremendous satisfaction in finishing a restoration knowing that the decisions were deliberate rather than accidental.

A successful restoration begins long before the first coat of paint.

It begins with a plan.
