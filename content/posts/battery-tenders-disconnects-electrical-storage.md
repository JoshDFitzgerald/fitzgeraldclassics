Last week we walked through [winter storage in general](/post/winter-storage-done-right). This week I want to slow down on the one item that causes more springtime frustration than everything else on that checklist combined: the battery.

A classic car can go into the garage in November running perfectly and come out in March with a battery that will not take a charge. Nothing broke. The battery simply sat there, slowly discharging, and every week it sat low did permanent damage.

The good news is that this is one of the easiest problems in old-car ownership to prevent. You just have to understand what is happening while the car sits.

## A Sitting Battery Is a Discharging Battery

A lead-acid battery loses charge all by itself, even with nothing connected to it. That is called self-discharge, and it happens faster in heat than in cold.

That last part surprises people. Here in Arizona, summer garage temperatures are harder on a stored battery than a northern winter is. A battery that might lose a few percent of its charge a month in a cool garage can lose considerably more in a hot one.

Add whatever the car itself draws, and a battery can be in trouble in a matter of weeks.

Once a battery sits at a low state of charge, sulfation begins on the plates. That is the real killer. A battery can survive being run down once or twice. Sitting discharged for months is what shortens its life for good.

So the whole game is simple: keep the battery charged, or disconnect it so completely that nothing can drain it. Everything below is about choosing between those two strategies — or using both.

## A Battery Tender Is Not a Trickle Charger

These two get confused all the time, and the difference matters.

An old-fashioned trickle charger pushes a small, constant current into the battery whether the battery needs it or not. Leave one connected all winter and it can slowly overcharge the battery, boil off electrolyte and shorten the life of the very battery you were trying to save.

A modern smart maintainer — the Battery Tender brand is where the generic name came from — works differently. It charges the battery, monitors it, and then drops into a float or maintenance mode that only replaces what the battery loses. When the voltage dips, it tops it back up. It is designed to stay connected for months.

If a charger does not specifically say it has an automatic float or maintenance mode, do not leave it on a stored car unattended.

## What to Look for in a Maintainer

You do not need to spend much, but a few features are worth having:

- **Automatic float mode.** This is the whole point. Non-negotiable for storage.
- **Spark-proof and reverse-polarity protection.** Sooner or later everyone touches the wrong terminal. Good units shrug it off.
- **The right battery type.** If your car runs an AGM battery, the maintainer needs an AGM mode or AGM compatibility.
- **Enough output for the job.** A small 1-amp unit is fine for maintaining a healthy, charged battery. If the battery also needs to be brought back up from a partial charge, a 3- to 5-amp unit does it faster and can maintain just as gently.
- **A permanent ring-terminal lead.** Most good maintainers include one. Bolt it to the battery once, route the plug somewhere reachable, and connecting the maintainer becomes a ten-second job you will actually do.

## The Case for a Disconnect Switch

A disconnect switch does exactly what the name says: it physically separates the battery from the car. No draw, no drain, no guessing.

The common styles are a knob-type switch that mounts right on the battery terminal, and a lever or blade style mounted nearby. Either works. What matters is that it interrupts the ground side of the circuit — on a negative-ground car, that is the negative cable. On a positive-ground car like my 1957 Nash Metropolitan, the ground cable is the positive one. Disconnect the ground side, whichever polarity that happens to be.

A disconnect makes the most sense when:

- The car will sit for months with no access to an outlet for a maintainer.
- You want the electrical system dead while you work on the car.
- The car has a slow drain you have not tracked down yet — a disconnect buys you time, though it does not fix the problem.
- You simply like the added security of a car that cannot be started without opening the hood.

There are trade-offs. You lose your clock and any radio presets. On a mostly original car, a bright modern switch on the battery can also look out of place, though the knob style is easy to remove for a show. And a disconnect does nothing about self-discharge — a battery disconnected in November still needs a charge check before spring, because it has been slowly discharging into itself the whole time.

My own preference, when an outlet is available: a smart maintainer as the primary plan, because it keeps the battery full rather than merely slowing the loss. The disconnect is the backup, and the right answer for cars stored away from power.

## Parasitic Draw: The Drain You Cannot See

Parasitic draw is anything in the car that keeps sipping power after the key is off.

On a truly original classic, the honest answer is: almost nothing. A mechanical clock is about it, and many of those only wind themselves in short bursts. If your original car kills a battery in two weeks, something is wrong — a glovebox or trunk light staying on, a voltage regulator sticking, a worn ignition switch, a chafed wire touching ground.

Modern additions change the picture completely:

- A modern radio with station memory draws power constantly.
- Alarms and keyless-entry systems draw constantly.
- USB chargers and phone accessories can draw even with nothing plugged in.
- Electronic fuel injection and modern ignition boxes have memory functions that sip power around the clock.

Individually these draws are small — often 20 to 50 milliamps. But do the math: a 50-milliamp draw pulls more than an amp-hour out of the battery every day. In a few weeks, a stored battery is in the damage zone.

## How to Check for Draw

You do not need much equipment. A basic multimeter is enough.

1. Charge the battery fully and let it sit a few hours so the surface charge settles.
2. Make sure everything is off — doors closed, lights off, key out.
3. Disconnect the ground cable and connect the meter in series between the cable and the battery terminal, set to measure milliamps.
4. On an original car, you should see nearly zero. A reading above roughly 50 milliamps on a car with modern accessories deserves a closer look.
5. If the draw is high, pull fuses one at a time and watch the meter. When the reading drops, you have found the circuit. Then find the component.

One warning: do this patiently, and never crank the engine with a multimeter connected in series. The meter is only there to measure the small stuff.

## What Changes With a Restomod

If your classic has modern electronics — EFI, an alarm, a modern stereo, electric fans with controllers — treat the battery question as mandatory rather than optional.

Those cars almost always have some parasitic draw, and their electronics are happier with stable voltage anyway. A smart maintainer is close to a requirement, and if you also fit a disconnect, understand that cutting power may reset learned settings, clocks and presets every time you use it.

None of that is an argument against the upgrades. It is just part of the bargain, and it is easily managed once you know it exists.

## My Storage Routine

For a car going away for the season, mine looks like this:

- Battery fully charged before storage, terminals cleaned and inspected
- Smart maintainer connected with the ring-terminal lead, cord routed so it cannot chafe or be tripped over
- If no outlet is available: battery disconnected at the ground cable, and a calendar note to check its charge mid-storage
- Cables and terminals checked for corrosion — it does not improve while the car sits
- Nothing left plugged into the cigarette lighter or accessory socket
- A quick voltage check before the first start of spring, rather than discovering a weak battery by grinding the starter

## The Goal

A battery is not an expensive part, but that is not really the point.

The point is that a classic stored properly should wake up ready — turn the key, hear it crank strong, and go. A $40 maintainer and ten minutes of attention in the fall are what make that happen, instead of a jump pack, a parts-store run and a battery that never quite recovers.
