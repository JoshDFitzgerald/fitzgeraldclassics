Why the Details Matter More Than You Think

There is something incredibly satisfying about opening the hood of a freshly restored classic car.

The engine is clean.

The hardware looks new.

The wiring is neatly routed.

The paint shines.

Everything looks exactly the way you imagined it would when the restoration began.

But there’s an interesting question that every serious restorer eventually has to ask:

## Does it look new—or does it look correct?

Those aren’t necessarily the same thing.

I’ve encountered this repeatedly while restoring my 1966 Corvette.

At first, engine painting seems simple enough. Find the correct Chevrolet Orange, prepare the engine properly, spray it and move on.

Then you begin researching how these engines were actually assembled and painted at the factory.

Suddenly you’re asking questions you never expected:

Should this bolt be orange?

Was the intake manifold installed when the engine was painted?

Should there be overspray here?

Was this bracket installed before or after painting?

Were the exhaust manifolds painted?

Should everything be perfectly covered?

And one of the strangest lessons of all:

Sometimes making an engine less perfect can make the restoration more correct.

Welcome to the fascinating world of factory engine finishes.

## Restoration Versus Re-Creation

When most of us first begin restoring cars, our natural instinct is to make everything beautiful.

Strip it.

Prime it.

Paint it.

Make every surface perfect.

That approach produces a gorgeous engine.

But automotive factories weren’t restoration shops.

They were production facilities.

Engines moved down assembly lines.

Components were installed in predetermined sequences.

Paint was applied quickly and efficiently.

Nobody stopped the production line because a little orange overspray landed somewhere it shouldn’t.

Nobody masked every bolt head so it would look perfect at a Saturday morning car show 60 years later.

The objective was manufacturing consistency and corrosion protection—not concours judging.

Understanding that distinction completely changes how you approach a historically accurate restoration.

## Start With the Correct Color

Obviously, the first question is determining what color the engine should actually be.

That sounds easy.

Sometimes it is.

Sometimes it isn’t.

Manufacturers changed engine colors between years, factories, engine families and applications.

Even something as familiar as Chevrolet Orange has generated decades of discussion regarding the exact shade.

Ford enthusiasts encounter similar debates over different shades of blue.

Chrysler restorers deal with multiple engine colors depending upon engine family and production period.

Other manufacturers present their own challenges.

Before buying paint, establish:

Model year

Engine

Horsepower/application

Assembly plant when relevant

Production date

Factory documentation

Accepted judging standards if the car will be judged

Don’t choose a color because an internet photograph “looks right.”

Lighting, cameras, computer screens and previous restorations make photographs surprisingly unreliable references.

## My 1966 Corvette Taught Me This Lesson

My Corvette has its numbers-matching 327/350-horsepower small-block.

During the restoration, I’ve spent considerable time researching seemingly insignificant details because those details collectively determine whether an engine compartment merely looks good or actually looks authentic.

The cylinder heads on my engine, for example, are both casting number 3782461, with period-appropriate casting dates.

The engine uses the familiar Winters aluminum intake manifold.

That combination creates an interesting restoration detail.

The aluminum intake itself wasn’t simply painted Chevrolet Orange along with everything surrounding it.

Factory assembly and painting practices resulted in characteristic areas of paint and overspray around components.

That’s exactly the kind of detail that’s easy to erase during a modern restoration.

If we simply mask everything perfectly and paint each component individually, we may create something prettier than Chevrolet produced.

But we’ve also removed part of the manufacturing history.

## Overspray Isn’t Necessarily a Mistake

This concept takes some getting used to.

We’re trained to think overspray equals poor workmanship.

In modern collision repair, it usually does.

In factory restoration, overspray can be evidence of the original assembly process.

Imagine an engine moving through production.

Certain components are already installed.

The engine is sprayed.

Paint naturally lands on adjacent surfaces, fasteners and edges.

That tells us something.

It tells us when components were installed relative to when the engine was painted.

In other words, paint patterns can actually reveal assembly sequence.

That’s why serious restoration research goes far beyond asking:

“What color was the engine?”

The better question is:

## “What did the engine look like immediately after the factory painted it?”

Those are very different questions.

## Don’t Paint Every Part Separately

This is probably one of the easiest ways to accidentally over-restore an engine.

Imagine completely disassembling an engine and individually refinishing every component.

Each bolt is beautiful.

Every bracket is perfect.

Every edge is masked.

Everything is then assembled carefully.

The finished engine may be spectacular.

But if the factory painted many of those components after assembly, your restoration won’t replicate the original appearance.

Correct restoration sometimes requires thinking like the factory.

What was installed first?

What was painted together?

What remained natural?

What received overspray?

What was installed afterward?

Once you start approaching restoration this way, engine painting becomes almost archaeological.

## Natural Metal Is a Finish Too

Not every component under the hood was painted.

Some pieces were:

Bare aluminum

Cast iron

Cadmium plated

Zinc plated

Phosphate coated

Black oxide

Natural steel

Other factory finishes

One of the biggest visual mistakes in an engine compartment is painting something simply because bare metal looks unfinished.

Sometimes unfinished is the finish.

Of course, this creates another challenge.

Bare metal begins oxidizing.

That’s where restoration becomes a balancing act between authenticity and preservation.

Depending upon the component and restoration objective, restorers may use appropriate protective treatments that preserve the natural appearance without making the part look obviously coated.

Again, the goal isn’t merely making something attractive.

It’s making it believable.

## Hardware Tells the Story

Bolts are another fascinating detail.

If a bolt was installed before an engine was painted, its head may have received engine paint.

If it was installed afterward, it may retain its original plated or natural finish.

That means something as insignificant as an orange bolt head can help establish assembly sequence.

This is why I strongly recommend photographing and documenting original engines before stripping them—particularly cars that haven’t previously undergone major restoration.

Even ugly photographs can contain valuable information.

That greasy bolt you’re tempted to clean may be preserving evidence that disappeared from thousands of other examples decades ago.

## Don’t Trust the Previous Restoration

Here’s another trap.

Just because something was a certain color when you disassembled the car doesn’t mean the factory painted it that way.

Your classic has lived an entire life before reaching your garage.

Previous owners painted engines.

Mechanics replaced components.

Dealerships performed repairs.

Engines were rebuilt.

Accessories were added.

Someone may have painted everything under the hood black in 1978 because that’s what they liked.

Existing paint is evidence, but it isn’t automatically proof.

Look underneath it.

Search protected areas.

Compare factory documentation.

Look at unrestored examples.

Consult recognized marque experts and judging standards.

The objective is to build a body of evidence rather than relying upon a single clue.

## The Danger of Over-Restoration

This may sound strange coming from someone who loves beautifully restored cars, but an engine compartment can actually become too perfect.

Perfectly polished aluminum.

Mirror-finish hardware.

Every clamp identical.

Every bolt freshly plated.

Absolutely zero overspray.

Every painted surface possessing modern show-car gloss.

It’s beautiful.

But does it resemble something that left an American automobile factory in the 1960s?

Maybe not.

There’s nothing wrong with building a show car that exceeds factory quality if that’s your objective.

But call it what it is.

A historically accurate restoration attempts to reproduce the automobile.

A custom restoration attempts to improve it.

Both can be exceptional.

They’re simply different objectives.

## Build a Paint Map Before You Start

One technique I’ve found increasingly useful is creating what I think of as a paint map before refinishing an engine.

For each component, determine:

## Color

## Finish

## Gloss level

## Whether it was installed during engine painting

## Whether overspray should be present

## Whether hardware was painted or natural

## Whether the component was installed afterward

This doesn’t have to be complicated.

A notebook, photographs and a simple spreadsheet work perfectly.

The important part is making these decisions before the spray gun comes out.

Research is considerably easier than removing the wrong paint later.

## Fitzgerald Insight

The more restoration work I do, the more I’ve come to appreciate imperfections.

Not damage.

Not sloppy workmanship.

## Authentic imperfections.

A little factory overspray.

A bolt head that isn’t perfectly covered.

A casting texture that hasn’t been sanded smooth.

An aluminum component that looks like aluminum instead of polished jewelry.

Those details remind us that these automobiles weren’t originally built to become museum pieces.

They were built to be driven.

Men and women assembled them on production lines.

Engines were painted quickly.

Cars were inspected.

Keys were turned.

And somebody drove them away.

Our job as restorers isn’t necessarily to erase every trace of that manufacturing process.

Sometimes our job is to recreate it.

That’s one of the great ironies of high-quality restoration:

## It can take considerably more work to make something authentically imperfect than perfectly beautiful.

## Before You Paint Your Engine

Do the research first.

Determine the correct color.

Understand the factory assembly sequence.

Identify which components were already installed.

Research hardware finishes.

Study original photographs.

Look at unrestored cars whenever possible.

Document everything you discover.

Then decide what kind of restoration you’re building.

If your goal is a beautiful driver, you have considerable freedom.

If your goal is historical accuracy or judged competition, those small details become increasingly important.

Neither approach is wrong.

What matters is making the choice intentionally.

Because once everything is covered in fresh paint, some of the evidence may be gone forever.
