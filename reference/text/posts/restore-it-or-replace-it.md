Why the ugliest part on your classic car may be the one most worth saving

One of the easiest things to do during a classic-car restoration is throw something away.

A bracket is rusty.

A piece of hardware looks terrible.

A carburetor is covered in decades of grime.

A switch doesn't work.

A piece of trim is scratched and dull.

You open a catalog, discover that a shiny new replacement is available, and suddenly the decision seems obvious.

Why spend hours cleaning and rebuilding a 60-year-old component when a brand-new one can arrive at your door next week?

I've become increasingly cautious about making that decision.

Working on my 1966 Corvette has taught me a lesson that I think every restorer should learn as early as possible:

## Never throw away an original part until you know exactly what it is.

Because underneath the grease, rust and faded paint may be something you simply can't buy from a catalog.

It may be a piece of your car's history.

## New Doesn't Necessarily Mean Better

We have never had greater access to reproduction parts.

That's a tremendous advantage for our hobby.

Without the companies manufacturing replacement wiring harnesses, weatherstripping, interior components, lenses, trim, mechanical parts and thousands of other items, many classic automobiles simply couldn't be restored.

I'm grateful those parts exist.

But there's an important distinction between available and equivalent.

A reproduction component may look nearly identical in a photograph yet differ from the original in subtle—and sometimes not-so-subtle—ways.

The casting may be different.

The stamping may be missing.

The plating may have the wrong appearance.

The contours may be slightly off.

Mounting holes may require persuasion.

Threads may not be as precise.

The chrome may not have the same depth.

And sometimes the original component was simply manufactured better.

That doesn't make reproduction parts bad.

It means we shouldn't automatically assume that replacing something is an improvement.

## My Corvette Changed the Way I Look at Old Parts

My 1966 Corvette is a numbers-matching car, and that changes the restoration equation considerably.

As I've worked through the car, I've learned to look for information before reaching for the parts catalog.

Casting numbers.

Stamped numbers.

Date codes.

Manufacturer markings.

Original finishes.

Even components that initially appear unremarkable can tell part of the car's story.

An alternator isn't necessarily just an alternator.

A cylinder head isn't simply a cylinder head.

A distributor isn't merely something that makes the spark plugs fire.

On the right car, those components can help establish whether the pieces surrounding the engine today are consistent with the automobile that left the assembly plant more than six decades ago.

Once an original component disappears into a trash can, that history may disappear with it.

And you can't order provenance from a catalog.

## The First Rule: Don't Clean Away the Evidence

There is another trap.

Sometimes we're so anxious to restore a part that we accidentally erase the very information we need to identify it.

Before sandblasting, wire-wheeling, grinding, polishing or painting an old component, inspect it carefully.

Look for:

Casting numbers

Stamped identification numbers

Date codes

Manufacturer logos

Inspection marks

Paint markings

Assembly stamps

Part numbers

Original plating

Unusual hardware

Evidence of original finishes

Take photographs before doing anything.

Take more photographs than you think you'll need.

Photograph the component installed on the car.

Photograph its orientation.

Photograph the hardware.

Photograph wire routing.

Photograph brackets and spacers.

Then photograph every marking you can find.

Digital photographs cost virtually nothing.

Trying to remember six months later which direction an obscure bracket faced can cost an entire evening in the garage.

Ask me how I know.

## Restoration Begins With Identification

When I remove an unfamiliar component, I've increasingly adopted a simple process:

## Identify first. Restore second. Replace third.

That order matters.

Suppose you remove an old component and discover a casting number.

Researching that number may reveal that the part is correct for your model.

A date code may then reveal that it was manufactured during an appropriate period before the car's assembly.

Suddenly the rusty part sitting on your workbench isn't merely an old part.

It's potentially an original or period-correct component.

Now the question changes from:

"Where can I buy a new one?"

to:

"Can I save this one?"

That's a much better question.

## What Does "Original" Actually Mean?

This is where restoration terminology can become confusing.

A part can be several things.

Original to the car means there is credible reason to believe it has been on that particular automobile since production.

Period correct means the component is appropriate for the vehicle's production period but isn't necessarily the piece originally installed on that particular car.

Correct replacement generally means it matches the proper specification but may have been produced later.

Reproduction means a newly manufactured component designed to replicate the original.

Those distinctions may matter enormously on one automobile and very little on another.

If you're restoring a historically significant, highly documented or judged automobile, originality can be extremely important.

If you're building a dependable weekend cruiser, a high-quality reproduction may be the smartest choice.

Neither approach is inherently wrong.

The important thing is understanding which decision you're making.

## When I Would Restore the Original

There are several situations where I strongly favor restoration.

If the component is identifiable as original or correctly dated to the car, I want to preserve it whenever reasonably possible.

If the reproduction is visibly inferior, I'd rather repair the original.

If the original part fits properly and can be made mechanically safe, that's another strong argument for restoration.

And if the component contains markings or manufacturing characteristics that can't be reproduced accurately, preservation becomes even more attractive.

Things such as:

Original castings

Carburetor bodies

Distributors

Alternator housings

Starter housings

Original radios

Certain brackets

Factory trim

Original wheels

Switches

Gauge assemblies

can often be rebuilt rather than discarded.

A component doesn't have to remain mechanically untouched to retain historical value.

Sometimes preserving the original housing while rebuilding the internals gives you the best of both worlds.

## When Replacement Is the Better Decision

Originality should never become an excuse for compromising safety.

Some components deserve replacement when deterioration has made them unreliable or dangerous.

Brake hoses are an obvious example.

So are severely corroded brake lines, structurally compromised suspension pieces, unsafe tires, damaged bearings and deteriorated electrical wiring.

There are also situations where repairing an original component simply isn't practical.

The part may be damaged beyond reasonable repair.

Critical material may be missing.

A previous owner may have modified it beyond recovery.

Or the cost of restoration may be wildly disproportionate to the significance of the component.

That's when a quality reproduction becomes invaluable.

The goal isn't to preserve every rusty bolt at any cost.

The goal is to make an informed decision rather than an automatic one.

## Safety, Reliability and Originality Can Coexist

I don't believe a classic automobile has to become unreliable simply because we care about originality.

There are thoughtful ways to preserve appearance and history while improving usability.

My Metropolitan radio project is a perfect example.

The radio looks appropriate for the car, but internally it uses modern electronics.

My Corvette's original-style ignition appearance can coexist with electronic ignition hidden inside the distributor.

Modern internal components can sometimes make an original assembly more dependable without visually transforming the vehicle.

I like modifications that make a car safer or more enjoyable while respecting what the automobile is.

There's a difference between improving a classic and erasing its character.

## Create a "Do Not Throw Away" Box

Here's one of the simplest pieces of advice I can give anyone beginning a restoration.

Get a large box.

Label it:

## ORIGINAL PARTS — DO NOT DISCARD

Everything removed from the car goes into that box until you've identified it.

Even if you ultimately install a reproduction component, keep the original when practical.

Why?

Because restoration standards change.

Better rebuilding techniques become available.

Replacement parts go out of production.

Future owners may value something you don't.

And sometimes, months later, you discover that the seemingly insignificant bracket you almost threw away is incredibly difficult to find.

Storage space is cheap.

Irreplaceable original parts aren't.

## Don't Forget the Hardware

This lesson applies to nuts, bolts, washers, clips and fasteners too.

During a restoration it's tempting to replace every piece of hardware with shiny new material.

Sometimes that's appropriate.

Sometimes it makes the finished restoration look less authentic.

Original automotive hardware often has distinctive head markings, finishes and dimensions.

Before replacing it, determine whether the original hardware can be cleaned, replated or otherwise restored.

At minimum, keep it as a reference.

A $1 bolt may contain information that helps you determine what a correct $1 replacement should look like.

## The Economics Can Surprise You

There's another reason to investigate before replacing.

Sometimes restoring an original component actually costs less.

You may already own the expensive part.

It just doesn't look expensive yet.

A weekend spent cleaning, disassembling, researching and refinishing something can transform what appeared to be scrap into a beautiful original component.

Of course, your time has value.

But for many of us, that work is part of why we restore cars in the first place.

There is a tremendous sense of satisfaction in reinstalling a component and knowing:

## This is the same piece that was on this automobile decades ago.

You can't get that feeling by opening a shipping box.

## Fitzgerald Insight

I've noticed something changing in the way I approach restoration.

Years ago, when I saw a tired component, my first thought might have been:

## "I need a new one."

Today my first thought is:

## "What is it?"

That's a small change in thinking, but it can completely change a restoration.

An old automobile is a collection of clues.

Casting numbers, date codes, fasteners, finishes and even apparently insignificant components help tell us what happened to the car during its life.

Our responsibility as restorers isn't simply to make everything shiny.

It's to understand what we're looking at before we change it.

Sometimes restoration means replacing something.

Sometimes it means rebuilding it.

And occasionally the best restoration decision is to clean a component carefully, preserve what remains and leave history alone.

## Before You Throw Anything Away

The next time you're standing beside the trash can holding a rusty old component, stop for thirty seconds.

Ask yourself:

## Do I know exactly what this is?

## Have I checked it for numbers or markings?

## Is it original or period correct?

## Can it be rebuilt?

## Is the reproduction actually better?

## Will I wish I still had this five years from now?

If you can't confidently answer those questions, put the part back on the bench.

You can always throw something away tomorrow.

You may never be able to get it back.

That's one of the most important lessons these old cars continue to teach me.

Restoration isn't simply about making the past look new again.

Sometimes it's about recognizing which pieces of the past were worth preserving all along.
