Why the dashboard deserves the same attention as the paint, engine and chrome

There is a point in almost every restoration when the project finally starts feeling like a car again.

For some people, it’s when the freshly rebuilt engine fires for the first time.

For others, it’s when the body comes out of the paint booth.

For me, one of those moments happens when the dashboard starts going back together.

Think about it.

When you’re driving a classic automobile, you can’t see the beautiful paint on the hood. You can’t see the restored chassis underneath you. You certainly can’t see all the hours you spent cleaning and detailing the engine compartment.

But you spend every mile looking at the dashboard.

The steering wheel is in your hands.

The gauges are directly in front of you.

Your fingers operate the original switches and knobs.

The radio, heater controls, chrome trim and instrument lighting create much of the experience that separates driving a 60-year-old automobile from driving something built last year.

That’s why I believe a dashboard deserves much more attention than simply making it look new.

It should make the car feel right.

## Before Removing Anything, Take Pictures

This may be the single most valuable piece of advice in this article.

Before dismantling a dashboard, photograph everything.

Then photograph it again.

Take wide photographs showing the entire dashboard.

Move closer.

Photograph every switch.

Every knob.

Every gauge.

Every wire.

Every ground.

Every bracket.

Every clip.

Every bulb socket.

Every cable.

Then get underneath the dashboard and take photographs from angles you probably won’t understand until six months later.

You’ll be glad you did.

A dashboard can contain an astonishing number of small components, and many appear obvious when you’re taking them apart.

They become considerably less obvious when they’re sitting in labeled bags six months later.

## Don’t Assume the Dashboard Is Original

A car that’s 50 or 60 years old has had plenty of opportunities to accumulate changes.

Someone may have installed an aftermarket radio in 1978.

A switch may have been replaced.

A previous owner may have drilled a hole for an accessory.

The gauges may have been repaired.

Wiring may have been modified.

The dash may have been repainted.

Before automatically reproducing what you find, determine whether what you’re looking at is actually factory correct.

This is where restoration becomes detective work.

Factory manuals, assembly documentation, period photographs and unrestored cars can be invaluable.

Your car tells you a story.

But sometimes previous owners have edited a few chapters.

## Preserve the Original Components Whenever Possible

One lesson I’ve learned repeatedly during restoration is that an ugly original part may be considerably more valuable than a beautiful reproduction.

That applies particularly well to dashboards.

Original:

Switches

Knobs

Bezels

Gauges

Radio components

Heater controls

Trim

Fasteners

Brackets

can often be cleaned, rebuilt or refinished.

Before ordering replacements, determine whether the original can be saved.

Reproduction components are enormously important to our hobby, and sometimes replacement is absolutely the right decision.

But fit, lettering, plating, contours and tactile feel can differ.

There’s something satisfying about turning a switch and knowing it’s the same switch someone operated when the car was new.

## Label Every Wire

If you’re replacing the entire wiring harness, you might think labeling the old wiring is unnecessary.

Do it anyway.

The original harness gives you valuable information about:

Routing

Connection points

Branch locations

Ground locations

Retaining clips

Component orientation

I like simple labels that identify both the wire and its destination.

Don’t write:

## “Red wire.”

Write:

## “Headlight switch — terminal X.”

That distinction can save considerable frustration later.

And photograph the connection before disconnecting it.

Documentation and labeling work together.

## Grounds Deserve Special Attention

Old automobiles frequently develop electrical problems that aren’t caused by failed components at all.

They’re caused by poor grounds.

Paint, corrosion, rust and loose fasteners can interfere with electrical paths.

Ironically, restoration itself can create grounding problems.

You beautifully paint a dashboard structure.

You refinish a bracket.

You powder-coat a component.

Then you assemble everything and discover that an electrical component no longer has the metal-to-metal contact it originally relied upon for grounding.

Suddenly the horn doesn’t work.

The gauge behaves strangely.

The dash lights are intermittent.

When restoring electrical components, always understand how the circuit completes its ground path.

Pretty paint and electricity aren’t always friends.

## Gauges: Restore Before Replacing

Classic gauges deserve their own discussion—and they’ll receive one in the next Fitzgerald Classics feature—but they’re an important part of dash planning.

Don’t automatically assume an old gauge is defective because it looks tired.

Gauge faces can sometimes be restored.

Movements can be repaired.

Senders can be tested.

Bezels can be refinished.

Lenses can be cleaned or replaced.

And specialized shops can often restore an original instrument while preserving its authentic appearance.

Before replacing anything, identify exactly what you have.

That principle seems to come up repeatedly in restoration.

There’s a reason.

## Be Careful Cleaning Gauge Faces

This deserves special mention.

A gauge face that has survived six decades can be surprisingly delicate.

Lettering and markings may not tolerate aggressive cleaners.

The same product you’d confidently use on a modern plastic component can permanently damage an original gauge face.

Start with the least aggressive cleaning method possible.

Test inconspicuous areas.

Never assume.

The objective isn’t to make a 60-year-old component look like it was manufactured yesterday.

The objective is to make it look like a beautifully preserved example of what it actually is.

## Pay Attention to Gloss

One of the easiest ways to make a restored dashboard look subtly wrong is using incorrect gloss levels.

Not every black surface was high-gloss black.

Not every painted surface matched the exterior finish.

Manufacturers deliberately used lower-gloss finishes in certain areas to reduce reflections and glare.

A dashboard painted in beautiful modern high-gloss paint may photograph wonderfully while becoming annoying when sunlight reflects directly into your eyes.

Research:

Color

Gloss

Texture

Application method

Paint correctness isn’t simply choosing the right color code.

Finish matters too.

## Chrome and Brightwork Can Transform the Interior

Small pieces of dashboard brightwork have an outsized visual impact.

A beautifully restored bezel surrounding a gauge can make the entire instrument panel look better.

The opposite is also true.

One heavily pitted piece of chrome can suddenly become the only thing you notice.

Before sending everything out for replating, however, determine what was actually chrome.

Some components may have been:

Polished stainless

Plated zinc

Cadmium

Painted

Anodized

Natural metal

Making something shinier than the factory intended isn’t necessarily restoration.

Again, research before refinishing.

## Test Everything Before Installation

This is another lesson worth repeating.

Before installing the dashboard, test every component you reasonably can.

Test:

Switches

Bulbs

Gauges

Radio

Heater controls

Wiper system

Blower motor

Cables

Clock

Courtesy lighting

It’s much easier to diagnose a problem while the component is sitting on the bench.

Once the dashboard is assembled, accessing certain parts may require undoing hours of work.

There’s nothing enjoyable about removing a freshly installed instrument cluster because one tiny bulb doesn’t illuminate.

Ask me how I know.

## Restore the Dash as a System

It’s tempting to treat dashboard restoration as a collection of individual parts.

Gauge.

Switch.

Radio.

Knob.

Trim.

But the dashboard works best when treated as one interconnected system.

Before final assembly, think through:

## What gets installed first?

## What becomes inaccessible afterward?

## Where does the wiring need to route?

## Which fasteners need to remain loose until another component is positioned?

## When should the steering column be installed?

## When does the radio go in?

## Can I reach the grounds after the cluster is installed?

Creating an assembly sequence can prevent a surprising amount of unnecessary disassembly.

## Resist the Urge to Modernize Everything

Modern technology offers enormous temptation.

Digital gauges.

Touchscreen stereos.

USB ports.

LED displays.

Modern switches.

Some of those upgrades can be useful.

But I always ask myself whether an improvement adds functionality without changing the experience of the automobile.

A hidden USB charging port may make perfect sense.

A modern radio concealed behind an original-looking face can provide the best of both worlds.

Replacing an entire classic instrument panel with a giant digital display is a much bigger decision.

There isn’t one correct answer.

It’s your automobile.

But think carefully about what you’re gaining—and what you’re giving up.

A classic dashboard is one of the strongest connections between the driver and the era in which the automobile was built.

Once that’s gone, it’s difficult to recreate.

## Don’t Forget What You Can’t See

Some of the most important dashboard restoration work disappears completely once the project is finished.

That’s okay.

Restore and protect:

Hidden brackets

Grounding points

Wiring retainers

Defroster ducts

Heater cables

Firewall seals

Grommets

Insulation

Nobody may ever compliment you on them.

But they’ll appreciate the absence of rattles, electrical problems, water leaks and mysterious noises.

Sometimes the best restoration work is the work nobody notices.

## Fitzgerald Insight

I’ve come to think of the dashboard as the handshake between the automobile and the driver.

It’s where we interact with the machine.

We don’t experience a classic car through its paint code or casting numbers while we’re driving.

We experience it through the steering wheel, gauges, switches, pedals, sounds and even the glow of the instrument lights at night.

That’s why I don’t believe a dashboard should merely look restored.

It should preserve the experience the designers intended.

Every time we replace an original switch with something generic, drill another hole or modernize something simply because we can, we change that experience a little.

Sometimes the change is worthwhile.

Sometimes it isn’t.

The important thing is recognizing that we’re making a choice.

The best restorations I’ve seen aren’t necessarily the ones with the most new parts.

They’re the ones where everything feels like it belongs.

## The Moment It Comes Together

Eventually the wiring disappears behind the dash.

The gauges return to their openings.

The bezels go back on.

The knobs are installed.

The steering wheel returns.

You connect the battery.

Turn on the lights.

And for the first time in years, the dashboard glows again.

That’s one of those restoration moments worth remembering.

Because you’re not simply looking at a collection of restored components.

You’re looking at the automobile beginning to wake up.

And eventually, you’ll sit behind that dashboard, turn the key and drive the car.

That’s what all those photographs, labels, tiny screws and hours spent upside down beneath the steering column were really about.
